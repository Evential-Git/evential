# Evential website audit

Audit date: August 26, 2026

Scope: every public page was rendered in full at 1440px desktop and 390px mobile. This records the baseline issues found before the redesign in this working pass, so some items below are now resolved. It covers visual hierarchy, narrative, credibility, imagery, diagrams, interaction, responsive behavior, and commercial clarity. It deliberately evaluates the website rather than the product.

## The central problem

The site contains unusually good raw material: a real deployment, named measured exhibitors, original trade-show photography, a published measurement rule, an honest failed-deployment account, and an actual report artifact. But it presents that material like documentation instead of a commercial story.

The strongest website story is:

1. An organizer sells a booth before the show.
2. Evential makes performance visible during and after the show.
3. The exhibitor receives evidence, the organizer sees what to improve, and both have a better renewal conversation.
4. Over time, the same measurement layer can connect sponsor discovery, physical engagement, and repeat investment.

The current site repeatedly interrupts that story with hardware descriptions, feature grids, FAQs, pricing mechanics, and three different product identities.

## What this redesign changed

- Reframed the homepage around exhibitor renewal, organizer confidence, and decisions—not a feature inventory.
- Unified the primary navigation and commercial path: How it works → Results → Sponsor Discovery → Pricing → Vision.
- Preserved the EVENTIAL logo wordmark in Montserrat Alternates everywhere.
- Added a prominent, moving rail using the ten measured NCAEC exhibitors' real landscape marks, with accessible roster labeling and restrained optical sizing.
- Added connected diagrams for signal flow, event operations, the performance-report decision chain, sponsorship lifecycle, Method timelines, Engagement Index, pricing, future venue architecture, sponsor-contact graph, and funding visibility.
- Promoted real NCAEC photography and a report artifact into the main narrative.
- Reworked interior-page heroes and hierarchy so the pages read like one editorial B2B site.
- Recast the Indianapolis page as an engineering note rather than equivalent customer proof.
- Removed contradictory “free dashboard,” monetary “ROI report,” and automatic-renewal language.
- Removed the broken report-email form and linked directly to the real NCAEC PDF.
- Added a ten-year Vision page based on the value-pillars conversation, with explicit labels separating current capability from future direction and clear privacy guardrails.
- Reduced mobile repetition with horizontal media/diagram rails and more compact operational layouts.

## Site-wide issues

### Positioning and value articulation

1. The company alternates between “exhibitor performance measurement,” “event analytics,” “sponsor ROI,” and “sponsor discovery.” A visitor cannot tell which is the company and which are supporting capabilities.
2. Sponsor Discovery appears beside floor measurement as an equal product, but the site never explains the strategic loop between finding a likely sponsor and proving the sponsorship later.
3. The site describes what the system records more often than why an organizer buys it.
4. The primary emotional stakes—renewal anxiety, sponsor trust, defending the floor to a board, fixing bad placement, and retaining revenue—arrive too late or as small body copy.
5. The main audience is inconsistent. Some copy addresses organizers, some sponsors, some venue operators, and some investors.
6. “Sponsor” and “exhibitor” are used as if interchangeable. They often overlap, but the distinction matters in event-industry language.
7. “ROI report” implies monetary return while the Method page correctly says Evential does not calculate monetary value. “Performance report” or “engagement report” is more credible.
8. “Free dashboard” followed by “pay $3.99 per attendee” reads as contradictory. The software may have no subscription, but event coverage is not free.
9. “Every exhibitor gets a report” is too absolute beside a proof point where ten of 40+ booths were measured. It should be framed as the coverage outcome, not a claim about the pilot.
10. “Next year sells itself” overpromises. Measurement supports a renewal decision; it does not make renewal automatic.
11. The site rarely says what changes for the buyer the week after an event.
12. The site has no explicit long-term thesis, so Sponsor Discovery and future data ambitions feel disconnected from the current product.

### Narrative and reading experience

13. Pages are assembled from interchangeable “eyebrow + heading + paragraph + three cards” sections.
14. Sections have similar visual weight, so proof, explanation, qualification, and minor detail all feel equally important.
15. Most pages read as inventories of features rather than a sequence of buyer questions.
16. Headings frequently describe the section (“The hardware,” “How it works,” “Questions”) rather than carrying a meaningful outcome.
17. The site uses too many centered headings. Centering reduces scanability on long B2B pages and weakens the editorial voice.
18. Long pages lack chapter navigation or progress cues.
19. Repeated closing navy CTA bands make pages feel templated.
20. Repeated FAQs are used to carry information that belongs in the main narrative.
21. The content does not consistently distinguish current capability, demonstrated capability, example interface, and future vision.
22. Negative qualifications are sometimes visually louder than the value they protect.

### Visual system

23. The revised typography and neutral palette are stronger, but the lower pages still inherit many small, generic icon tiles from the earlier system.
24. Product diagrams use tiny line icons where a spatial or causal diagram is needed.
25. Mock interfaces are visually low-fidelity compared with the photographed proof. They can make the real system look conceptual.
26. Light-blue UI chrome appears repeatedly and begins to feel like a template.
27. Several information blocks are surrounded by borders even when simple type and spacing would be clearer.
28. The page-hero treatment on interior pages is a large rectangle inside another large rectangle. It looks like a slide, not a website masthead.
29. Interior heroes use too much empty horizontal space and not enough visual evidence.
30. Cards and diagrams have inconsistent radii, shadows, and background temperatures.
31. Some photography is presented as a small card when it should be a full-width emotional moment.
32. Product cutouts on pale backgrounds feel less premium than the real in-field photography.
33. The footer is dense, small, and visually detached from the more confident new homepage typography.
34. The navigation does not expose the Event Dashboard even though it is a major product page.
35. The navigation has no path to the company vision or strategic story.

### Images and diagrams

36. The ten measured exhibitors are shown as a paragraph/list. They should be a prominent moving or wrapping exhibitor rail.
37. The homepage repeats hardware photography but does not show the complete system in one frame.
38. “What your team has to do” should be an event-operations timeline, not four text rows.
39. Badge → gateway → dashboard → report should be one connected signal diagram, not separate cards across several sections.
40. The commercial outcome should be a second diagram: discover → sell → measure → prove → renew.
41. The report needs an annotated explanation showing which figures change which decisions.
42. Crowd flow needs a venue map/heatmap illustration, not only a grid of colored squares.
43. The Method page needs a threshold timeline showing a walk-by, a qualified dwell, and a repeat visit.
44. The Engagement Index needs a simple 65% dwell / 35% repeat-visits composition diagram.
45. Pricing would benefit from a live cost example or visual equation instead of another three-icon flow.
46. Events Measured should use a photographic project grid; a nearly empty national map emphasizes how few deployments exist.
47. The Indianapolis case has no visual evidence at all. The clock placeholder makes it feel unfinished.
48. The NCAEC photography section is too far down the case study and captions are too small.
49. The site has enough real NCAEC images for a strong editorial mosaic, but most remain underused.
50. The future Pixel/gateway architecture belongs on the Vision page as a clearly labeled future-system diagram.

### Mobile

51. The homepage is roughly 11,200 pixels tall at 390px. That is excessive for the amount of distinct information.
52. Dashboard and Discovery are roughly 8,700px and 7,700px tall on mobile.
53. Small cards stack into long undifferentiated lists.
54. Mobile typography becomes too small in captions, notes, tables, and footer links.
55. Several diagrams simply turn into vertical icon lists, losing the relationships they were meant to explain.
56. Repeated full-width CTA bands create visual whiplash.
57. The mobile footer occupies too much height relative to its usefulness.
58. On the NCAEC case, charts and result copy become too small to parse comfortably.
59. The exhibitor roster becomes an especially long low-emphasis text list.
60. The strongest report artifact is readable, but it appears after too much preceding explanation.

62. The buyer is not identified immediately as a trade-show organizer.
63. Two equal-weight CTAs create indecision; the proof path should be primary and the sales path secondary.
64. The NCAEC proof strip is strong, but it does not show the names/marks of the companies actually measured.
65. The first proof section repeats the same event facts from the strip.
66. The first quote is valuable but lacks a face, association mark, or editorial context.

68. The three process photos explain mechanics but do not connect signal to decision.
69. The privacy note interrupts the process section instead of being integrated into the diagram.

71. “What happens when a booth underperforms” is one of the best outcome sections but appears as another split block.
72. The live dashboard appears before the site has clearly explained why live intervention matters commercially.
73. The dashboard heatmap is presented as an example but visually resembles a finished product screenshot.

## Event Dashboard page issues

78. The hero promises a live dashboard but the overlapping artifact is a post-event sponsor report.
79. The hardware cards make the page begin with implementation instead of decisions enabled.
80. The setup flow is useful, but the connection from live signal to action is represented by generic icons.
81. Six feature blocks are too many and give equal emphasis to proven and aspirational capabilities.
82. Claims about safety alerts, waitlist releases, and automations require explicit “current/example/future” status.
83. “Better events make sponsors easier to keep” should be the page’s central argument, not a late split section.
84. The feed mock is cleaner than the heatmap, but neither demonstrates an actual floor or decision.
85. The page lacks a single worked example: signal detected → organizer action → sponsor outcome.

## Sponsor Discovery page issues

86. The page visually resembles a separate startup.
87. The strategic connection to measurement only appears near the bottom.
88. The core differentiator—“who is most likely to sponsor this specific event, and why?”—is not stated sharply enough.
89. The current table mock uses generic companies even though real sponsorship-history evidence would be more convincing.
90. Contact ranking, geography, title clusters, and evidence confidence are underexplained.
91. The contact-profile mock has too many tiny UI labels and too little explanation of why a person ranks first.
92. The “profile ends with a person” band repeats the same point instead of proving it.
93. Discovery should be presented as the beginning of the sponsorship measurement loop, not a neighboring tool.

## Pricing page issues

94. Pricing is visually clearer than the other subpages, but the comparison gives Discovery equal weight to the core coverage product.
95. “Floor measurement” does not say what outcome is purchased.
96. The $3.99 rate lacks immediate worked totals for common event sizes.
97. The NCAEC modeled economics are in a gray paragraph block instead of a visual calculation.
98. “You pay per badge” is clear but consumes an entire large section to explain one sentence.
99. Enterprise sizing is hidden in a small navy strip.
100. The page does not explain what happens if badges fail, attendees no-show, or only part of the floor is covered until the FAQ.

## Events Measured page issues

101. “Two floors, measured” foregrounds small scale rather than evidence quality.
102. The large US map dramatizes only two locations and makes the company look earlier than necessary.
103. The NCAEC case deserves dominant photographic treatment and direct result metrics.
104. The Indianapolis deployment is responsibly honest but should be labeled as an engineering note, not an equal customer proof card.
105. “2027 founding events” is sales language without qualification criteria or what a founding organizer receives beyond reports.

110. The traffic comparison chart is too small on both desktop and mobile.

## Indianapolis engineering-note issues

120. The page honestly documents failure, which is valuable, but the hero is a generic light panel with no event image or network diagram.
121. The opening paragraph is dense and defensive.
122. A timeline of gateway uptime would explain the problem faster than prose.
123. The two surviving findings are under-visualized.
124. The page should be explicitly named an engineering note so it cannot be mistaken for a sponsor-performance case study.

## Method page issues

125. The Method page is trustworthy but visually resembles terms and conditions.
126. The first two definitions are long bordered blocks without diagrams.
127. A reader has to mentally simulate time gaps, dwell duration, repeat visits, and overlap.
128. The 65/35 Engagement Index formula is buried in prose.
129. The “what we do not measure” statement is repeated and takes more space than the method summary.
130. The dispute process is good trust material but visually generic.
131. The changelog lacks version links or affected-report references.
132. The page needs a compact downloadable method specification for technical buyers.

## Legal and utility pages

133. Privacy and Terms are serviceable but use the same broad marketing header/footer density as commercial pages.
134. Privacy should more visibly distinguish anonymous badge signal, identity linkage, consent, sponsor sharing, and retention.
135. Redirect pages and the 404 should not expose outdated product names or dead visual patterns.

## Recommended information architecture

Primary navigation:

- How it works
- Results
- Sponsor Discovery
- Pricing
- Vision

The Event Dashboard should be explained as part of “How it works,” not as a competing top-level identity. Method should remain accessible from proof, privacy, reports, and the footer.

Homepage sequence:

1. Emotional/commercial outcome
2. Real proof and measured-exhibitor rail
3. The renewal problem
4. Capture → Prove → Improve system diagram
5. NCAEC result
6. Annotated report and decisions
7. In-field photography
8. Long-term measurement-loop teaser
9. Qualified sales CTA
